Wendy H Labs Timeline: Narrative Summary

The timeline tracks six hormone markers for Wendy from April 2021 through September 28, 2026. It combines MyChart result trends with older values that were recovered from scanned pages by text recognition (OCR). The steady markers are DHEA sulfate and free testosterone. DHEA has stayed in a narrow, normal band between about 115 and 160 ug/dL the whole time, and the latest reading is 121 against a range of 19 to 205. Free testosterone sat between 2.0 and 3.2 pg/mL for five years. Estradiol and progesterone are the markers that move. Estradiol swings widely from test to test: 107 in early 2023, 465 in April 2024, 61 that October, 385 in April 2025, 101 in August 2025, just 8.2 in April 2026, then back up to 176 on September 28. Progesterone was low, under about 1.3 ng/mL, through 2024. It rose to around 5 in 2025 and then to about 17 in both 2026 draws. The most recent draw shows the sharpest change. Total testosterone went from 23 to 114 ng/dL, and free testosterone went from 3.2 to 11.7 pg/mL. Both are now flagged high, at roughly double to triple the top of the reference range. Pregnenolone was measured once, at 122, which is near the top of normal.

Three trends stand out. First, the adrenal markers (DHEA and the single pregnenolone reading) look stable and normal. Second, progesterone has climbed in steps over about two years and has held at a much higher level since early 2026. Third, estradiol keeps swinging between high and low, and testosterone jumped sharply in the latest test. For a woman in her late fifties, that pattern looks more like hormone therapy being started, changed, or timed differently around blood draws than like the body's own production drifting. The September 2026 testosterone jump is the change most worth acting on, because it is new, large, and outside the normal range.

The practical takeaway is to bring the September 28 results to whoever manages Wendy's hormones. Ask what she was taking and at what dose before each draw, and whether a testosterone or DHEA product was added or increased around mid-2026. Ask whether her doses should be adjusted and when she should retest. Watch for signs of too much testosterone, such as acne, oily skin, hair thinning or new facial hair, irritability, or voice changes. The estradiol swings may simply reflect when each sample was drawn relative to a dose, so testing at a consistent time would make future results easier to compare. Two caveats apply. The pre-2026 numbers came from OCR, so a few may be misread. And this summary describes trends, not a diagnosis; her clinician should interpret it.
